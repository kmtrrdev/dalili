===== CLEANED GUIDE TEXT (24,712 characters) =====

Riyadh (الرياض ar-Riyāḍ) is the capital of Saudi Arabia, slightly east of the center of the country in the heart of the Tuwaig escarpment.

Understand

Known by local wags as the Dead Center of the Kingdom. With few sights of interest and a brutal climate, Riyadh is a business-only destination if there ever was one, but with the arrival of increasing entertainment options like movie theatres, concerts and conventions under Crown Prince MBS, that impressions is slowly changing. Only a few days visit will give you insights into the continuing collision of tribal Wahhabi conservatism grappling with modern technology and Western influences. 

History

 

Until the 16th century, Riyadh was known as "Hajr", an important city of central Arabia dating from at least the 3rd century CE (and probably older).  In the Middle Ages, Hajr served as the capital of the province of Al-Yamamah, whose viceroys presided over most of central and eastern Arabia.

With the decline in the fortunes and wealth of central Arabia after the 10th century, the name Al-Yamama gradually disappeared, and Hajr became subsumed under the name "Najd", which had referred only to areas lying further west.  The name Hajr also gave way to the name "Ar-Riyadh" (meaning "the gardens"), as the old city broke into several towns and farming estates.

Riyadh (or, rather, the hamlet of Diriyah, historically the next-door neighbour) is the ancestral home of the al-Saud family, who founded the Emirate of Diriyah in 1727. The nascent emirate was crushed by Ottoman forces led by Ibrahim Pasha in 1818, and what was left of the population fled to Riyadh. Driven out from Riyadh as well by the Rashids in 1891, the young King Abdulaziz ibn Saud famously left exile in Kuwait and raided and recaptured the city in 1902. This conquest was the beginning of what is now considered the "third Saudi state", which continues into the modern Kingdom of Saudi Arabia.  

In the early 1900s, Riyadh was a dusty walled town of under 20,000 people surrounded by palm groves, fertile wadis, and a number of small villages.  The city was made the capital of Saudi Arabia when the country was born in 1932, and has grown explosively ever since then. In 2008, the city was estimated to have 5 million inhabitants; as of 2025, its population is 7.7 million people.

Orientation

Riyadh is vast and sprawling.  The main roads are King Fahd Rd (طريق الملك فهد tariq al-malek al-Fahd), which runs north to south across the city, and Makkah Rd (aka Khurais Rd), which runs west to east, intersecting at Cairo Square &mdash; which is actually just a cloverleaf interchange.

The modern business districts of Olaya (العليا, pron. Oleyah) and Suleimaniyah, containing most offices and better hotels, are to the north of Makkah Rd.  Here two of Riyadh's skyscrapers serve as handy orientation points: Faisaliah Tower (the pointy one) is towards the southern end of Olaya, while Kingdom Centre (the bottle opener) is at the northern end. Both are located between King Fahd Rd and the parallel thoroughfare of Olaya Rd, which is Riyadh's main upscale shopping strip.

The historical core of Riyadh is to the south of Makkah Rd.  The district of al-Murabba hosts the sprawling grounds of the King Abdul Aziz Historical Park, home to the National Museum and the Murabba Palace, while a kilometer to the south is the dense warren of al-Bathaa, host to the city's cheapest food, lodging and shopping and the hub of the minibus network.  Further south yet is Deira, centered on as-Sa'ah Square, which has souqs (traditional markets), the Masmak Fortress, the Governor's offices and, more morbidly, the execution grounds.

See

 
 
 

Sightseeing in Riyadh requires a lot of transportation across long distances, and it can be unbearably hot to visit outside locations during the daytime. However, most sites stay open late at night and are often lit to be enjoyed mainly at night.

Museums and historical sights


* Diriyah. Riyadh's top sight is the  of Diriyah, the ancestral home of the Saudi royal family, which has been renovated to an inch of its life. It's located on the northwestern outskirts of Riyadh only about 15 km from the city center and is covered in a separate article.
* 
Masmak Fortress. The heart of old Riyadh, this was the fortress stormed by King Abdul Aziz and his men in their daring reconquest of Riyadh in 1902. Renovated in 2008 to an inch of its life, the mud brick structure now looks like it was built yesterday, but the museum inside does a pretty good job of recounting the story of the raid and has some fascinating photos of old Riyadh as well. Alas, the second half is devoted to extolling the greatness of the Sauds in everything from agriculture to education.

* 
Murabba Palace. Riyadh's second old mud-brick palace, built by King Abdul Aziz after he conquered Masmak Fortress and figured he should built something harder to conquer. This two-story structure does indeed look pretty intimidating, but permits are no longer needed to venture inside, where you can find sights including the first royal Rolls-Royce.

* 
National Museum. This museum (opened in 1999) is done up with the latest technology and is very accessible to visitors, with almost everything available in English. There are several video presentations and mini-theatres, so you could probably spend a lot of time here doing virtual tours of Madain Salih or watching re-enactments of the Prophet Mohammed's battle of Medina. Highlights include a kiswah cloth that once covered the Kaaba in Mecca. Half the time, though, it feels more like a propaganda exercise than a museum: the display on plate tectonics starts with a quote from the Quran, the history of the Sauds is rather airbrushed, and the display on the birth of Mohammed, reached from the clash and noise of the Jahiliyah (age of ignorance) by riding an escalator up into a room of soothing, pastel light while a choir of angels sings, has probably inspired a few conversions to Islam. Many cabbies will not recognize the English name: ask for the neighboring Murabba Palace (Qasr al-Murabba) instead.

* 
Royal Saudi Airforce Museum. Presents a history of Royal Saudi Airforce and Saudi Aviation throughout the years. Most prominent attraction is the L-1011 Lockheed TriStar, which serves as a gate guard for the museum and can be seen while on the road.


Other


* 
As-Sufaat. Next to the Great Mosque and the mutawwa headquarters, this nondescript expanse of cement is known by expats as Chop-Chop Square as convicts are publicly beheaded with a scimitar here. Public executions are rare since MBS came to power.  Executions used to take place on Friday mornings at 9AM, but not every week, and no notice was given in advance. Beware that any Westerners nearby have been known to be taken to the front row and forced to watch the whole thing, in order to further shame the condemned. It is forbidden to take photos of executions or to record videos of them.

* 
Kingdom Centre. Undoubtedly Riyadh's most stunning piece of modern architecture, at 305 m the Kingdom Centre is the second tallest building in Saudi Arabia and quite a sight, especially when lit up at night. The centre hosts an (expensive) three-story shopping mall, with one floor reserved for women, but the main reason to visit is the 99th-floor Skybridge connecting the two peaks at a height of 300 m. Best visited at dusk or after dark, from here you'll get great views over the vast and flat but well-lit expanse of the city.

* JAX District. A relatively new art district built at the edge of town close to Diriyah. Both international and Saudi artists exhibit their work here. Many works are aspirational about the "New Saudi" that is being built, but you will not find any artworks criticising the royal family or the current politics of the country.

Wadi Hanifa

A dry and sharply defined riverbed (wadi) begins about 40&nbsp;km north of Riyadh and runs in a north-south direction for over 120&nbsp;km, cutting through the western edge of the city, known as Wadi Hanifah.  Wadi Hanifah was once the lifeblood of the Riyadh area, rich in groundwater, filled with palm groves and farms and dotted with a string of small towns and villages throughout history. The Wadi has been used as a large dumping ground for wastewater, sewage, and industrial waste, but an ambitious rehabilitation project has been completed.  An 80&nbsp;km stretch running through western Riyadh is now essentially an 80-km desert park, though many parts of the Wadi floor are occupied by private estates and farms with high walls.  The Wadi has several entry points, but perhaps the easiest route is by taking King Abdullah Road west past the university and into the town of Arqah.  Eventually, you will reach a large round-about.  Take the exit heading downwards into the Wadi.  Follow the road even as it winds and weaves its way through the Wadi (do not be tempted to turn onto any side streets).  Eventually, you will reach a police checkpoint, to the left of which is an entry point to the Wadi floor.  A narrow paved road runs along the Wadi floor. Heading southwards, you will eventually find designated picnic and barbecue spots facing the Wadi's cliff-like walls.

While dry for most of the year, wadis can flood very quickly with a moderate amount of rain.  Never approach a wadi during the rain or even its immediate aftermath.  Even looking over the edge of a wadi can be dangerous as the Wadi's edges can break off during the rain. Every year, several deaths are reported from flash floods all across Saudi Arabia. The wadi is also home to packs of wild dogs, and hikers and bicyclists alike have reported discomfort with staying in the wadi after sunset due to the howling hounds.

Do


Desert hikes

West of Riyadh lies the edge of the Tuwaig Escarpment, also known as The Edge of the World. This is the main attraction around Riyadh, and should not be missed if you have the time for a half-day trip out of the city. The ideal way to visit the Edge is with a tour operator that takes you out to the Edge for the sunset. Often you will also get semi-traditional Saudi cooking served Arab picnic-style by the Edge. These tours can often be quite pricey, and the Visit Saudi website will claim that the route to the Edge is closed, but this is not true. At least on the weekends, you can take a private vehicle (make sure you have a suitable 4WD vehicle, and a phone with GPS) out to the Edge from Huraymila. Most of the way is dirt road that is almost indistinguishable from the surrounding desert, and be aware that fatal accidents have occurred by the Edge.

Taking the Makkah Road west for 30 minutes, you can drive down the Tuwaig escarpment and drive down to the lowlands that are Najd-proper. As you make the 200-m sharp drop down the steep road, you will get a good feel of the desert with dunes and buttresses. On a clear day, you can also see the imposing pillar known as Faisal's Finger. There are camps for horse-riding, as well as sand dunes where you can hire a 4x4 and go "dune bashing", or hire a camel for a short ride and some scenic photos. Be prepared for aggressive salespeople, but relish in the opportunity to haggle.

Heading northeast of the airport to the Thumama sand dunes, one can engage in "dune bashing" in 4x4 SUVs or in rented ATVs.
* 
Red Sands Desert Tour. Several tour companies offer desert trips, mostly to Ad-Dahna desert 1 hour east of Riyadh near the road towards Dammam. Tours usually run from 2PM to 11PM and include drinks, food and activities such as volleyball, sand boarding, etc. (SR250). Some tours let you camp in the desert. Reliable operator include Yalla Hike, HikeMania and Riyadh Hiking.

Dirab Golf & Country Club. Nice layout with green and inviting grass, and the last 9 holes are even floodlit. They offer tennis, swimming and horse-back riding as well.

If you travel about 20 minutes to the north-east you will find a not so nice desert course with browns instead of greens (the putting area consists of a sand/oil mixture instead of grass).

King Fadh Sports City. This stadium has closed for reconstruction. It's being expanded to 92,000 capacity and will host soccer matches in the AFC Asian Cup Finals in 2027.

* 
KSU Stadium. Opened in 2015, this is the home ground of Al Nassr FC, who play soccer in Pro League the top tier. It has a capacity of 25,000 and in 2027 hosts matches in the AFC Asian Cup Finals.

* 
Prince Faisal bin Fahd Sport City. With a capacity of 22,500, this is the home ground of Al Hilal and Al Shabab, who both play soccer in Pro League. It too is a venue for the 2027 AFC Asian Cup Finals.

* Prince Mohammed bin Salman Stadium and Riyadh Stadium are two new venues under construction.

Events

You can enjoy one of several cinema complexes that show the latest Western, Arabic, and Indian movies; the movies shown are censored though. (Check the VOX Cinema website for detailed information of movie listings and locations.)

From October to April; the city celebrates "Riyadh Season" which several open air activities become available due to the cooler temperatures, these include: Boulevard World, Winter Wonderland, and Wonder Garden. 

Festivals

 

The main festival event in Riyadh is Soundstorm, organised by MDLBeast the first week of December. The focus is dance music and big spectacle DJs, but artists like Metallica, Muse and Eminem have also played there. Tickets can be found on nofomo.com  

As part of the modernisation efforts in the country, there are occasional concerts or other activities happening in the country, but mostly only during Riyadh Season. Use the service Webook to look for events.

The Jenadriyah festival (الجنادرية al-Jenadriyah, also Janadriyah, Janadria etc) is a two-week-long festival of Saudi culture held every year between January and March around 45 km from Riyadh. The event was on hiatus between 2020 and 2025, but reopened in 2026 as a part of the Custodian of the Two Holy Mosques Camel Festival, a major camel race.

Buy

Most if not all of Riyadh's main attractions have at least a partial shopping element. To really see this strange city, you need to visit the many malls and shopping areas where people go to congregate and prove to themselves and others that Saudi Arabia is indeed a modern, wealthy country with all the trappings of any other industrialized society.

Traditional markets (souqs)

* 
Souq al-Thumairi. Also known as Antique Souq, this is Riyadh's most touristy souq, which isn't saying all that much.  It specializes in Arabic goods cheap and expensive, authentic and fake, with carpets, coffee pots, daggers, jewelry and more. Most of it is made elsewhere, nothing is authentically Saudi. English is generally spoken, and haggling is obligatory.

* Souq al-Uwais (سوق العويس), King Fahd district. Also known as The Yemeni Market, this is a typical souq that is much less touristy than Souq al-Thumairi. Here regular people come to haggle over furniture, carpets and clothes. It is also right next door to Souq al-Taibah, another large market where you can buy jewelry amongst other things.

Drink

With alcohol and nightclubs banned, Riyadh's nightlife is infamously non-existent. Despite that however, the Riyadh is much more lively and vibrant during the night as the temperature cools.  Shisha (water pipe) cafes, long banned, were finally allowed back within city limits from 2019 -- ask a local (or any taxi driver) for his favorite.  Coffee shops can also be found in abundance throughout the city, particularly on Tahlia St (officially Prince Mohammed Bin Abdul Aziz St) in Olaya.

For the foreign workers - the expats - the social life can be quite (well, comparatively) rich however. There are always a good party going on in the embassy area or in one of the compounds. And at these private parties there's always a chance to find some illegal booze.

There is a slowly growing market for "clubs" that seek to recreate the feeling of going out drinking, but without serving any alcohol. Entrance fees are usually exorbitant. Some speculate that these clubs will be the first places where alcohol will be served when it is finally allowed in the kingdom. Among these are OMNI Club in Tahliyah, Beast House in the JAX District and Attaché in the Diplomatic Quarter.

In case you run into it, especially within expat communities, Saudi champagne refers to a non-alcoholic drink, typically a mix of Sprite and apple cider.

* 
The Globe. Suspended 240 meters above Riyadh in the giant glass ball of the al-Faisaliah building, the Globe is the hippest cafe-restaurant and probably the single best splurge in town. So dimly lit at night that the waiters fade into the shadows, you can settle back in a plush leather seat, order a bottle of (non-alcoholic) bubbly, puff on a Cohiba and watch the lights of the city twinkle below. Reservations required, but they'll make one for you at the lobby if there's space. On the way out, stop at "the experience" level outdoor viewing platform.
